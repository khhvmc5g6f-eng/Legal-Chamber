# FTC

The third regulator profile in this repository, following `cnil.md`'s and `ico.md`'s format - see `README.md`. This profile covers the FTC's privacy and data-security enforcement role specifically, not its (much larger) antitrust/competition mission, which shares the same statute but a different enforcement history.

## Statutory basis

Per `ftc.gov/about-ftc/mission/enforcement-authority` (a Commission-published memo, revised July 2025, checked 2026-08-22): the FTC's core authority is **Section 5(a) of the FTC Act**, 15 U.S.C. § 45(a)(1): "unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce ... are ... declared unlawful." The Commission's own memo distinguishes the two prongs: "deceptive" practices are those involving "a material representation, omission or practice that is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably in the circumstances" (per the Commission's Policy Statement on Deception, referenced in the same memo); "unfair" practices are those that cause "substantial injury to consumers which is not reasonably avoidable by consumers themselves and not outweighed by countervailing benefits," per 15 U.S.C. § 45(n). There is no separate, freestanding "data security" or "privacy" statute underlying most FTC privacy enforcement; the Commission has built its data-security caseload almost entirely on Section 5(a) unfairness/deception theory, not a dedicated privacy statute. This is a structural difference from CNIL and the ICO, which each enforce a dedicated data-protection statute (the French Data Protection Act/GDPR, and the DPA 2018/UK GDPR respectively).

Sector-specific statutes verified live as also giving the FTC privacy/data-security enforcement power:

- **COPPA** - the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 6501-6505, implemented by the Children's Online Privacy Protection Rule, 16 CFR Part 312. Confirmed directly on the Rule's own FTC page (`ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule-coppa`, checked 2026-08-22), which states the Rule applies to operators of sites/services directed to children under 13, and to general-audience operators with "actual knowledge" they are collecting data from a child under 13.
- **GLBA** (Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act) - gives the FTC enforcement authority over the **Safeguards Rule**, 16 CFR Part 314 (information-security program requirement for covered financial institutions) and the **Privacy Rule**, 16 CFR Part 313 (customer notice/opt-out requirements). Confirmed on `ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/rules/safeguards-rule` and `ftc.gov/business-guidance/privacy-security/gramm-leach-bliley-act` (both checked 2026-08-22). The Safeguards Rule applies to "financial institutions subject to the FTC's jurisdiction and that aren't subject to the enforcement authority of another regulator under section 505 of the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act" - i.e. the FTC is a residual/backstop GLBA enforcer for financial institutions not otherwise assigned to a banking regulator, per 15 U.S.C. § 6805.
- The Commission's own "Statutes" index (`ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes`, checked 2026-08-22) states the agency "has enforcement or administrative responsibilities under more than 80 laws," including recent additions such as the TAKE IT DOWN Act (Pub. L. 119-12) and the Protecting Americans' Data from Foreign Adversaries Act of 2024 (Pub. L. 118-50) - both listed there as current law. This profile does not attempt to catalogue all 80+; it covers the privacy/data-security-relevant statutes verified above. Any other sector statute not named here is `UNVERIFIED` for this profile and should not be assumed covered.

## Investigation stages

Per `ftc.gov/about-ftc/mission/enforcement-authority` (checked 2026-08-22): pre-complaint investigations are "generally non-public." The Commission's general investigative authority comes from Section 6(a), 15 U.S.C. § 46(a) (authority to investigate the "organization, business, conduct, practices, and management" of an entity). For privacy/data-security matters specifically, the operative compulsory-process tool is the **civil investigative demand (CID)** under Section 20, 15 U.S.C. § 57b-1: "the Bureau of Consumer Protection may use only CIDs, rather than subpoenas, to investigate possible 'unfair or deceptive acts or practices.'" A CID can compel documents, testimony, tangible things, and written reports or answers to questions.

Per `ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2018/01/so-you-received-cid-faqs-small-businesses` (checked 2026-08-22, an FTC Bureau of Consumer Protection publication): a CID recipient must arrange a "meet and confer" with FTC staff, which "must take place no more than 14 days after you receive the CID." A recipient who wants the CID narrowed or withdrawn may, "within 20 days after you are served with the CID," file a petition to limit or quash under 16 C.F.R. § 2.10, decided by a single FTC Commissioner. Receiving a CID does not necessarily mean the recipient is the target; the Commission also sends CIDs to third parties holding relevant information. After staff reviews the response, the matter may be closed with no action, resolved by settlement, or proceed to a Commission decision to sue.

## Enforcement tracks

The FTC's structure is **not** a CNIL-style ordinary/simplified pair of tracks toward the same kind of sanction. It is two procedurally distinct routes serving different purposes, and per `ftc.gov/about-ftc/mission/enforcement-authority` (checked 2026-08-22) they can be used sequentially or in combination on the same underlying conduct:

- **Administrative adjudication.** When the Commission has "reason to believe" a violation occurred, it may issue a complaint under Section 5(b), 15 U.S.C. § 45(b). A respondent may settle by signing a consent agreement without admitting liability; the Commission then places the proposed order on the public record for a 30-day comment period before deciding whether to finalize it. If the respondent contests, the matter is tried before an **administrative law judge (ALJ)** under the Commission's Rules of Practice (16 CFR Part 3), with FTC "complaint counsel" prosecuting. The ALJ issues an initial decision; either side may appeal to the full five-member Commission, which hears briefs and oral argument and issues its own final decision and order.
- **Federal court litigation.** Independently of the administrative track, Section 13(b), 15 U.S.C. § 53(b), lets the Commission go straight to federal district court "whenever the Commission has reason to believe" a party "is violating, or is about to violate" a law it enforces, to seek a preliminary or (in "proper cases") permanent injunction, without first completing an administrative adjudication. This is the FTC's usual vehicle for a fast-moving data-security or privacy enforcement action, since it does not require waiting for the ALJ/Commission process to conclude.

## Sanction range

The available remedy differs sharply depending on the track and on whether the FTC is dealing with a first-time violator or a repeat one, and this area has genuinely changed since 2021:

- **Cease-and-desist order** (administrative track): the core remedy from a contested or consented administrative proceeding. It does not itself carry a monetary penalty for a first violation.
- **Civil penalties for violating a final order** - Section 5(l), 15 U.S.C. § 45(l): once a Commission cease-and-desist order becomes final, violating it is punishable by a civil penalty, assessed by a federal district court. Per `16 CFR § 1.98(c)` on eCFR (current text checked 2026-08-22, showing Title 16 "up to date as of 8/20/2026"), the inflation-adjusted maximum is **$53,088 per violation**, with each day of continued violation a separate violation. This figure was set by the FTC's own Federal Register rule effective 2025-01-17 (`federalregister.gov/documents/2025/01/17/2025-01361`, checked 2026-08-22) and, per the current eCFR text (last amended 8/01/2026), has **not** been superseded by a 2026 adjustment - it remains the operative cap as of the check date. (Note: a document titled "Civil Monetary Penalties-2026 Adjustment," filed 2026-01-14, was checked and found to belong to the Surface Transportation Board, not the FTC - flagging this because a keyword search initially conflated the two; it is not evidence of an FTC 2026 adjustment.)
- **Civil penalties against knowing violators who were never a party to the order** - Section 5(m)(1)(B), 15 U.S.C. § 45(m)(1)(B): same $53,088-per-violation cap, but requires the FTC to prove the violator had "actual knowledge that such act or practice is unfair or deceptive and is unlawful," typically shown by having given the violator a Notice of Penalty Offenses.
- **Consumer redress** - Section 19, 15 U.S.C. § 57b: after an administrative cease-and-desist order is final and *all judicial review of it is complete*, the Commission may sue in federal district court for consumer redress, but must show a "reasonable man would have known under the circumstances [the conduct] was dishonest or fraudulent." This route still exists post-2021 but is slow, since it requires the full administrative process plus appeal to run first.
- **Injunctive relief under Section 13(b)** - preliminary and permanent injunctions remain available in federal court. **Monetary relief under Section 13(b) does not.** In *AMG Capital Management, LLC v. FTC*, 141 S. Ct. 1341 (2021), the Supreme Court held unanimously that Section 13(b)'s injunction language does not authorize the FTC to obtain equitable monetary relief (restitution or disgorgement) - the FTC's long-standing primary tool for getting money back to consumers in first-time-violation cases, including data-security cases, without going through the slower Section 19 route.
- **Congress has not restored that authority as of the check date.** Per `schakowsky.house.gov/media/press-releases/schakowsky-menendez-clarke-reintroduce-legislation-restore-ftc-authority` (an official member-of-Congress press release, checked 2026-08-22): the Consumer Protection and Recovery Act, which would restore FTC authority to obtain monetary relief under Section 13(b), was most recently reintroduced as **H.R. 10003 (119th Congress) on 2026-07-30** and remains pending in committee (referred to Energy and Commerce and to Judiciary). The release itself states the House passed an earlier version "with bipartisan support" in the 117th Congress (2021-2022), but the Senate did not act and no version has been enacted. This is therefore live-verified current law, not assumed: the AMG Capital Management restriction on Section 13(b) monetary relief is still good law as of 2026-08-22.
- congress.gov itself returned a bot-verification block ("Just a moment...", a Cloudflare challenge) when fetched this session. Per `docs/OPERATING_RULES.md`'s block-handling rule, that block was treated as final and not routed around; the House-member press release above was used as a legitimate alternative primary source for the bill's current status instead.

## Appeal route and forum

Per `ftc.gov/about-ftc/mission/enforcement-authority` and the statutory text at `law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45` (both checked 2026-08-22): a respondent against whom the Commission has issued a final administrative order may petition for review under **Section 5(c), 15 U.S.C. § 45(c)**, filed with "the court of appeals of the United States, within any circuit where the method of competition or the act or practice in question was used or where such person, partnership, or corporation resides or carries on business," within **sixty days from the date of service of such order**. The statute states the Commission's factual findings, "if supported by evidence, shall be conclusive" - a substantial-evidence-type standard for facts; the statutory text as fetched does not itself spell out the standard applied to the Commission's legal conclusions, so that point is `UNVERIFIED` here rather than assumed. If the court of appeals affirms, it enters its own enforcement order; the losing party may then seek certiorari from the Supreme Court, per the same FTC memo.

For the judicial (Section 13(b)) track, a district court's grant or denial of an injunction is, as a general matter of federal civil procedure, appealable to the relevant U.S. court of appeals. This general point was not independently re-verified this session against an FTC-specific primary source describing that appellate path, so the *specific* procedural route for a 13(b) order (as opposed to the general principle that district court orders are appealable) is flagged `UNVERIFIED` rather than asserted as FTC-specific doctrine.
